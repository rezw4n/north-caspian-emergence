Projections of seabed emergence in a shrinking closed sea have two uncertain inputs, the fall of the water and the shape of the floor it uncovers. For the next metre or two of fall in the North Caspian, the second matters as much as the first. The primary pathways differ by decades in when the first metre of fall arrives; the grids differ by a factor of \metric{erl.text.h3.uncorrected.1.ratio} in how much seabed that metre exposes, and all five agree on where for only \metric{erl.agree.1m.all_pct_of_any}\% of it. An assessment built on one grid\citep{court2025,duan2025} inherits an error that a whole-sea check cannot reveal\citep{bocharov2026}, because the error lies in where the shoreline goes, not in the total area. That error matters most for what sits near the present shoreline: ports and channels, water intakes and the deltaic wetlands of the Volga and Ural. The spread across grids should not be read as an interval that contains the true emergence, because every grid misplaces the historic shorelines below the land-data epoch and the grids share sources.

Better estimates need seabed data in the zone that will emerge next: hydrographic surveys and charts where they exist, or photon-counting lidar bathymetry where the water is clear enough. ICESat-2's nearshore bathymetry product does not cover the Caspian\citep{atl24_v2}, so no independent check of that zone is available today. The waterline record offers a partial remedy: each metre the sea falls is surveyed by the next waterlines, so an audit like this one can be repeated as the sea falls, and grid heights replaced by observed ones.

The observations are Landsat only, at \metric{params.stage2_res_m}~m: Sentinel-2 was left out of the main run to stay within the computing budget, and the pre-registered wind-setup rule selected no screening because the waterline scatter it measures stayed at one grid cell for every threshold. Independent noise floors, the falling-limb audit and the passing control argue against observation error, and per-sector datum shifts do not remove the grid errors (Supplementary section~\SIredteam). The Komsomolets Bay level departs from the basin level, so errors there may partly be an artefact of the level pairing. The second half of the audit hypothesis, larger errors in deltaic and vegetated sectors, depends on the rule that removes deep water misread as land and should be treated as unresolved. Several registered robustness variants could not be run within the time and computing budget, and the alternative thresholds, sensors and season rules were tested on the elevation model rather than on the audit (Supplementary section~\SIrobust).

Our waterline elevation model failed its pre-registered test, so the audit uses observed shorelines only, as pre-registered. On \metric{h2.atl08.n_test_blocks} held-out blocks of \metric{h2.block_size_km}~km, its NMAD against ICESat-2 land heights is \metric{h2.atl08.dem.nmad_m}~m, against \metric{erl.text.h2.atl08.product_nmad_min_m}--\metric{erl.text.h2.atl08.product_nmad_max_m}~m for the grids, but its root-mean-square error (RMSE; \metric{h2.atl08.dem.rmse_m}~m) is not lower than theirs (\metric{erl.text.h2.atl08.product_rmse_min_m}--\metric{erl.text.h2.atl08.product_rmse_max_m}~m), and the block-bootstrap \metric{params.ci_pct}\% intervals of the paired differences include zero for \metric{erl.text.h2.atl08.diff_nmad.n_ci_include_zero} (NMAD) and \metric{erl.text.h2.atl08.diff_rmse.n_ci_include_zero} (RMSE) of \metric{erl.text.h1.n_products} grids. It fails at the Volga delta front, probably because flooded reeds are classified as water, and in Komsomolets Bay, where the level pairing breaks down. With \metric{params.block_size_sens_lo_km}~km blocks it would pass against the ICESat-2 land and vegetation heights, so this verdict rests on few blocks. Its nominal \metric{erl.text.conformal_pct}\% intervals cover \metric{erl.text.pct.h2.atl08.coverage.overall}\% of test heights (block-bootstrap \metric{params.ci_pct}\% interval \metric{erl.text.pct.h2.atl08.coverage.overall_boot_low}--\metric{erl.text.pct.h2.atl08.coverage.overall_boot_high}\%), and the GLO-30 and GEDI checks give the same picture (Supplementary section~\SIdem).

Exposure is reported for infrastructure and one wetland class only; protected areas, Ramsar sites and Caspian seal habitat are not yet included.

The mechanism is not specific to the Caspian. Global grids are least constrained in shallow inland seas, where soundings are sparse and vertical references undocumented, and grids that fill recently emerged land from post-emergence elevation data will look skilful above the recent shoreline but not below it. Great Salt Lake reproduces both effects. The generality test covers one further basin, however: Lake Urmia was not run within the computing budget, and the control at Great Salt Lake is post-emergence lidar that shares the observed shorelines with the audit. Other shrinking closed basins, such as the Aral Sea and Lake Urmia\citep{yao2023}, are the natural next tests.
