A shrinking closed sea records its own floor, and the record can audit the maps used to project what it will expose next. In the North Caspian, \metric{erl.text.h1.n_products_majority} of five global bathymetry grids misplace three decades of observed shorelines by kilometres while matching their total areas, and the fifth comes close only where it holds land heights acquired after the seabed emerged. The same holds at Great Salt Lake. For the next metre of fall the grids disagree by a factor of \metric{erl.text.h3.uncorrected.1.ratio} on how much seabed emerges and almost entirely on where, and no correction of them can yet be validated. Projections of near-term emergence in shrinking closed seas are therefore limited by bathymetry, not only by the water-level pathway. They should report the spread across grids, and they need new seabed data in the band that will emerge next.
