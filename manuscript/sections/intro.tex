Water levels in inland seas and lakes will fall, often dramatically, through the twenty-first century as the climate warms\citep{prange2020}. Where a closed sea is shallow and its floor nearly flat, even a modest fall exposes large areas. Where that newly exposed seabed lies decides what is lost: harbours and navigation channels, coastal wetlands, and the breeding and feeding grounds of the sea's fauna.

The Caspian Sea is now living through this. By the end of 2024 its level had fallen to \metric{lit.ginzburg2025.level_end2024_bhs_m}~m in the Baltic Height System, below the 1977 minimum of \metric{lit.ginzburg2025.min1977_bhs_m}~m, after falling at \metric{lit.ginzburg2025.rate_2020_2024_cm_per_yr}~cm per year over 2020--2024\citep{ginzburg2025}. By the end of the century, CMIP6 models project a further fall of about \metric{lit.samant2023.ssp245_m}~m under SSP2-4.5 (inter-model range \metric{lit.samant2023.ssp245_lo_m}--\metric{lit.samant2023.ssp245_hi_m}~m) and about \metric{lit.samant2023.ssp585_m}~m under SSP5-8.5 (\metric{lit.samant2023.ssp585_lo_m}--\metric{lit.samant2023.ssp585_hi_m}~m)\citep{samant2023}.

Much of the coming emergence will happen in the North Caspian. This shelf has a mean depth of \metric{lit.akbari2020.nc_depth_lo_m}--\metric{lit.akbari2020.nc_depth_hi_m}~m and holds less than \metric{lit.akbari2020.nc_volume_share_pct}\% of the sea's volume\citep{akbari2020}. Every metre of fall there moves the water's edge across about \metric{lit.akbari2020.nc_area_per_m_km2}~\kmsq{} of seabed\citep{akbari2020}. Published estimates of how much of the shelf will emerge, and of what it will take with it, rest on global relief grids: ETOPO1 in one case\citep{akbari2020}, and GEBCO\_2022 for an assessment that found the largest projected declines would remove up to \metric{lit.court2025.mpa_loss_max_pct}\% of existing marine protected area coverage\citep{court2025}.

These grids are least certain exactly where the projections lean on them hardest. Global bathymetric compilations rest on sparse soundings: only about \metric{lit.weatherall2015.control_share_pct}\% of the non-land cells of GEBCO\_2014 were based on bathymetric control data\citep{weatherall2015}. On a shelf this flat, a small vertical error moves the predicted shoreline a long way horizontally. Yet the grids have not, to our knowledge, been tested against the seabed that has actually emerged. The one Caspian comparison we found worked at the scale of the whole sea, and found areas from a chart-based model within \metric{lit.bocharov2026.max_area_diff_pct}\% of satellite-observed areas\citep{bocharov2026}. That is a test in which errors of opposite sign along different coasts can cancel.

A falling closed sea surveys its own floor. Each cloud-free satellite image records where the water's edge lay, and satellite altimetry records how high the water stood that day, so every dated waterline is a contour of the seabed. Waterlines paired with water levels underpin intertidal topography mapped from tidal stages\citep{sagar2017,bishoptaylor2019}. In a closed sea the water level itself sweeps the shelf over years rather than hours. Since 1992 the Caspian has risen to a maximum in mid-1995 and then fallen by \metric{levels.dahiti.range_m}~m to its 2026 low, crossing the upper part of that range twice.
